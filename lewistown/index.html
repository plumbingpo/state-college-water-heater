How Lewistown Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, lift the T&P valve lever annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush once a year if their water is hard.

The age of your heater matters just as much. With regular maintenance, a tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts showing warning signs, like discolored hot water, popping noises, or moisture under the tank, it's time to plan a replacement. A planned replacement is cheaper and causes far less stress than scrambling for a new heater with water spreading across the floor.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip ignored for months can keep stressing the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement plus water damage. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

Common Water Heater Problems in Lewistown

Water heaters in and around Lewistown deal with a familiar set of stresses. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping you may hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Then there's age. When the protective anode rod wears out, usually somewhere in the second half of a tank's life, the tank begins corroding from within, and a drip from the bottom seam often shows up next. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Setup matters too. A home on a private well may see more sediment and occasionally a sulfur smell in the hot water, which a different anode rod often cures. A tankless unit in hard water needs regular descaling. And an older install getting a new heater may need code items that weren't required when it went in: an expansion tank, a properly routed relief valve line, a drain pan, or seismic straps where required. Every one of these problems can be fixed, and most are much cheaper to address early.
